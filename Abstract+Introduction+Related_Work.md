## Abstract

The building sector remains a major target for decarbonization and climate adaptation, yet passive solar design is still frequently implemented through static shading rules, steady-state envelope metrics, or reactive operational logic. Such fragmented treatments are increasingly insufficient for buildings exposed to anisotropic sky conditions, transient heat storage and release, and highly variable internal disturbances. This paper proposes a Unified Solar–Thermal–Adaptive (USTA) framework that reformulates passive solar design as a time-domain, climate-adaptive decision problem. The framework couples anisotropic solar admission modeling, reduced-order transient thermal dynamics, multi-objective passive design optimization, and predictive façade control within a single physics-informed pipeline. In contrast to conventional noon-based or purely geometric approaches, USTA explicitly captures diffuse sky structure, thermal lag, and anticipatory control requirements under variable occupancy. The resulting formulation supports warm-climate solar rejection, cold-climate solar storage and survivability analysis, adaptive operation for stochastic-use buildings, and transferable design synthesis across latitudes and façade orientations. By combining physical interpretability with computational efficiency, the proposed framework provides a unified basis for passive-first retrofit and new-building design under heterogeneous climatic and operational conditions. ([UNEP - UN Environment Programme](https://www.unep.org/topics/cities/buildings-and-construction))

## 1. Introduction

Buildings remain one of the largest contributors to global energy demand and carbon emissions. According to UNEP, the buildings and construction sector accounted for 34% of global energy demand and 37% of energy- and process-related CO2 emissions in 2022, which makes building decarbonization and climate adaptation a first-order research priority rather than a secondary design concern [1]. In this context, passive solar design has regained strategic importance because it can reduce operational dependence on mechanical systems while improving climate responsiveness at the envelope level. ([UNEP - UN Environment Programme](https://www.unep.org/resources/report/global-status-report-buildings-and-construction))

However, a central limitation of much conventional passive solar practice is that it still relies on static or quasi-static representations of solar action. A substantial body of work has shown the importance of shading devices for cooling reduction, daylight regulation, and visual comfort [2], [4], [5]. Yet a large portion of this literature still evaluates fixed devices or climate-specific configurations as design objects, rather than treating solar admission as a time-resolved process coupled to changing sky conditions and building response [2], [4], [5]. This limitation becomes especially important when diffuse radiation is non-negligible. The Perez sky model was developed precisely because short-time-step irradiance on tilted surfaces cannot be represented adequately by overly simplified diffuse-sky assumptions; it explicitly accounts for circumsolar brightening and horizon effects that matter for façade-level solar gains [3]. In other words, a shading decision derived from noon geometry alone may be geometrically intuitive but still thermally incomplete. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0038092X9090055H))

A second limitation is that façade-level solar decisions are often decoupled from the time-domain thermal behavior of the building itself. Thermal inertia is well known to shift and smooth heat flows, but its actual effect depends on climate, insulation, and use pattern, which makes steady-state reasoning inadequate for many design questions [6]. Recent reviews of resistance–capacitance (RC) modeling further show that reduced-order thermal networks are attractive because they preserve physical interpretability while remaining computationally efficient for transient prediction and control [7]. Earlier work on low-order thermal networks likewise emphasized that dynamic building behavior can be modeled efficiently through resistances and capacitances, avoiding the full computational burden of high-order simulation while retaining time-dependent heat storage effects [8]. These studies establish an important methodological point: once thermal storage and release matter, passive design can no longer be treated as a purely geometric problem. It must be formulated as a coupled solar–thermal problem. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1364032117312236))

A third limitation appears when buildings face irregular internal loads, stochastic occupancy, or climate-sensitive operational disturbances. Model predictive control (MPC) has become one of the most influential frameworks for advanced building control because it can optimize comfort and energy performance over a prediction horizon using explicit system models and constraints [9]. At the same time, the literature also makes clear that MPC adoption is still hindered by model availability, data requirements, and the mismatch between model accuracy and control usefulness [10]. In parallel, dynamic façades have attracted growing attention because static envelopes are inherently decoupled from dynamic environmental stimuli; recent systematic reviews show that dynamic façades can improve thermal comfort, daylight performance, and energy efficiency, but they are still often studied as component technologies rather than as parts of an integrated passive-first decision pipeline [11]. As a result, the current literature contains strong work on solar devices, strong work on thermal models, and strong work on predictive control, but these lines are still insufficiently unified. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1367578820300584))

This fragmentation is increasingly problematic under climate adaptation and resilience requirements. Recent work on thermal resilience emphasizes that buildings must be evaluated not only under normal operation but also under extreme weather and outage conditions, where passive survivability and habitable indoor duration become essential performance criteria [12], [13]. Yet resilience-oriented studies are often assessment-focused: they quantify safe hours, survivability, or thermal autonomy after a disruption, but they do not necessarily provide a unified design-and-operation methodology that links solar admission, thermal storage, and adaptive control in one transferable framework [12], [13]. This creates a methodological gap between resilience evaluation and passive design synthesis. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0360132323008338))

To address these limitations, this paper proposes a **Unified Solar–Thermal–Adaptive (USTA)** framework for passive solar design. The core idea is to move from **static shading** to **time-domain control-oriented passive design**. More specifically, the framework makes four contributions. First, it introduces a time-resolved solar admission layer based on anisotropic sky modeling, so that façade decisions are driven by short-time-step radiative conditions rather than by noon-based geometric heuristics [3]. Second, it couples solar admission to a 3R2C transient thermal representation, enabling the framework to capture storage, release, delay, and survivability effects with a physically interpretable reduced-order model [6]–[8]. Third, it extends passive design into the operational domain by integrating multi-objective passive optimization and MPC-based adaptive façade control, thereby bridging design-time trade-offs and run-time anticipatory response [9], [10], [14]. Fourth, it targets not a single building type or climate, but a transferable synthesis problem across warm climates, cold climates, variable-occupancy buildings, and cross-latitude design generalization. In this sense, the proposed framework is not just a better shading model or a better controller; it is a unified solar–thermal–control formulation for climate-adaptive passive-first buildings. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0038092X9090055H))

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews prior work on shading and solar-admission modeling, reduced-order thermal modeling and thermal inertia, and predictive control and dynamic façades. Section 3 formulates the proposed USTA framework. Section 4 presents the optimization and control pipeline. Section 5 evaluates the framework under cooling-dominated, heating-dominated, and variable-occupancy scenarios, followed by generalized design synthesis and sensitivity analysis.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Static shading design and solar-admission modelling

Solar shading has long been recognized as one of the most important passive means for reducing cooling load, mitigating glare, and improving façade energy performance. Review studies have documented a wide range of shading devices, simulation methods, and performance criteria across climates and building types [2]. Representative application studies have shown that fixed overhangs, fins, and spectrally selective configurations can reduce solar gains while balancing visual comfort and daylight access [4], [5]. This literature has been highly valuable for identifying façade-level trade-offs, but its dominant modeling pattern is still largely configuration-oriented: optimize a fixed device, compare a set of façade alternatives, or assess one climate-specific shading package. Such formulations are useful for local design decisions, yet they do not fully resolve the deeper problem of **time-varying solar admission under anisotropic sky conditions**. The Perez model is foundational here because it explicitly models diffuse irradiance on tilted planes under all-weather conditions, including circumsolar and horizon effects that simplified geometric reasoning tends to suppress [3]. Our work builds on this insight but moves further by embedding anisotropic solar admission inside a larger transient decision framework rather than treating it as a standalone façade calculation. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1364032115008667))

### 2.2 Thermal inertia, transient response, and reduced-order models

A parallel literature has established that building performance is strongly shaped by thermal inertia and by the timing, not merely the magnitude, of heat transfer. Review work by Verbeke and Audenaert shows that thermal mass can time-shift and flatten heat-flow fluctuations, although its performance varies substantially with climate, insulation, and building use [6]. More recent RC-model reviews emphasize that low-order resistance–capacitance models offer a practical compromise between physical meaning and computational tractability, which makes them especially suitable for fast simulation, early design, and control-oriented prediction [7]. Earlier studies on low-order thermal networks likewise argue that dynamic building behavior can be captured by reduced numbers of resistances and capacitances at low computational cost, in contrast to higher-order methods whose parameterization and runtime burdens are often prohibitive for design iteration [8]. These works provide the methodological foundation for using 3R2C-style state representations in passive design research. At the same time, much of the thermal-inertia literature stops at dynamic analysis or performance explanation. Our framework takes the next step by directly coupling thermal storage and release to time-resolved solar admission and adaptive façade decisions. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1364032117312236))

### 2.3 Predictive control, dynamic façades, and resilience-oriented operation

The building-control literature has demonstrated that MPC is one of the most systematic ways to coordinate comfort, energy use, forecasts, and operational constraints [9]. However, review studies also make clear that the practical deployment of MPC still depends critically on obtaining suitable control-oriented models and understanding the relationship between model quality, data requirements, and final control performance [10]. Meanwhile, research on dynamic façades has expanded rapidly. Recent systematic reviews describe dynamic façades as a promising response to the mismatch between static envelopes and dynamic external stimuli, with reported benefits in thermal comfort, energy efficiency, and daylight performance [11]. Yet this line of work often concentrates on façade technologies or actuation logic, while leaving the building’s internal thermal storage and survivability behavior only loosely connected to the façade decision process [11]. At the same time, resilience-oriented studies increasingly evaluate passive survivability, habitable hours, and outage performance under extreme events [12], [13]. These studies highlight an important shift in performance criteria, but they also reveal that resilience metrics are still often used as evaluation endpoints rather than as native objectives in a unified passive design-and-operation framework. Our work responds to this gap by treating adaptive control and resilience not as add-ons, but as integral parts of passive solar design. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1367578820300584))

### 2.4 Positioning of this work

In summary, existing studies provide strong but still separated answers to three different questions: how to reduce solar gains with façade devices [2]–[5], how to represent transient thermal storage and release efficiently [6]–[8], and how to optimize building operation under forecasts and constraints [9]–[11]. What remains insufficiently addressed is the **joint formulation** of these questions as one coherent passive-first decision problem. The proposed USTA framework is positioned precisely at this intersection. Rather than optimizing shading alone, simulating thermal mass alone, or controlling a façade alone, USTA unifies anisotropic solar modeling, reduced-order transient thermal dynamics, multi-objective passive design, and predictive adaptive operation within one transferable framework. This is the main distinction between our work and the existing literature. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0038092X9090055H))
